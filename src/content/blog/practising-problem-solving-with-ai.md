For much of human history, physical effort was part of daily work. People carried, lifted, walked, built, and worked with their hands. As machines took over more physical tasks and many jobs moved behind desks, movement became something we had to make time for deliberately. 

Today, many of us go to the gym, run, or play sports to get the activity our working lives no longer provide.

For me artificial intelegence raises a similar question about thinking: **if our work requires less independent problem solving, how will we keep practicing it?**

Engineers, anaylsts, designers, and other knowledge workers can now delegate substantial parts of a task to AI. It can generate code, propose solutions, analyze information and draft explanations.

But completing a task and developing/improving the ability to complete it are different things. Research is beginning to show that AI assistance can improve immediate results while reducing what people learn along the way.

## when completing the task replaces learning

Consider a software engineer investigating a software bug. Working through the problem involves forming hypotheses, checking evidence, testing explanations, and learning from mistakes.
An AI assistant can shorten that process by suggesting a fix. If the engineer applies it without understanding why it works, the issue may disappear while an opportunity to develop diagnostic skill disappear with it.

A [2025 study by researchers at Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon, The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778), explored this concerns. Researchers surveyed 319 knowledge workers and collected 936 examples of AI use.

Greater confidence in AI's ability to perform a particular task was associated with less reported critical thinking. Participants also described shifting their effort toward checking and integrating AI-generated outputs.

The study measured self-reported behavior, so it cannot establish that participants lost problem-solving ability. Nevertheless, it raises a practical question: when AI supplies the first explanation, the first solution, and the first judgment, how much independent reasoning do we still practice?

Reviewing a proposed answer would require substantial experties. We should also consider how people will acuire that expertise if they rarely work through problems themselves.

## Making room for cognitive practice

As physical effort became less embedded in many jobs, people needed other opportunities to remain active. If AI removes opportunities to practice important cognitive skills, we may need to preserve those opportunities deliberately.

The brain is not literally a muscle, and these studies do not establish a scientifically validated “mental workout.” They do, however, give us reasons to examine how we work.

A practical starting point is to build a few habits into ordinary tasks:

- Attempt the problem first. Write down your understanding, an initial hypothesis, and a possible approach before asking AI.

- Request guidance while learning. Ask for hints, questions, or feedback on your reasoning when developing a new skill.

- Try to explain the solution independently. Close the AI conversation and describe why the solution works, what it assumes, and where it might fail.

- Test against evidence. Check an original source, run an experiment, or examine an edge case.

Keep some practice unassisted. Occasionally complete a representative task without AI to identify what you understand and what needs more work.

Employers should also make room for this practice, too. Teams can protect opportunities for debugging, mentoring, design discussions, and explaining decisions. If speed is the only thing rewarded, taking time to understand a solution becomes harder to justify.

We might also need to make room for problem-solving outside working hours, much as we make time for physical exercise. A small personal project, a coding kata, or a practical problem we care about can give us space to build our own solution with limited AI involvement. We can decide on an approach, work through obstacles, and test our ideas before turning to AI for feedback.

These are my proposed ways to preserve opportunities for independent thinking. Their effectiveness as a long-term response to daily AI use still needs testing and verification.
